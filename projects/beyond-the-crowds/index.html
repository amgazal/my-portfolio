Beyond the Crowds

Exploring what drives national park visitation.

I enjoy visiting parks to observe nature. While staying with my aunt in the Bronx, I noticed how much park activity varied between the places I walked or took the bus to. I wanted to understand what might be behind those differences.

Those were often NYC municipal parks. This analysis looks at National Park Service units nationwide—a different set of parks, connected by the same question.

Purpose, attractions, access, size, and counting practices differ across these places. This comparison does not show that a designation causes higher visitation.

04 / What I learned

Useful associations, unanswered questions

A small regression benchmark tested designation and coordinates on 367 units, holding out whole units across 25 folds. Designation was more informative than coordinates alone.

Coordinates only
−0.005
Designation only
0.273
Both together
0.303

Mean R² on log-transformed mean annual visits; the training-mean baseline scored −0.016. The combined score varied across folds (SD 0.103). These are prediction checks—not original visit-count explanatory power, causal effects, or crowding forecasts.

What the counts leave out

Recreation visits are not unique visitors, and annual totals cannot tell us how crowded a trail feels on a particular day. Reporting methods and coverage change; current metadata also misses some reporting codes.

Nearby population, accessibility, acreage, and historical designation changes remain unresolved. Local entrance counts, trail use, and visitor experience would be needed before suggesting staffing or access changes.
